# U.ness

**Version 4.0 · Beta**

Free, local, private, energy-aware AI for knowledge work. No cloud, no subscription,
no account. Your prompts never leave your machine.

## What it is

U.ness runs open language models on hardware you already own. That single choice —
local instead of cloud — is what makes everything else possible.

**Free and accessible.** No subscription, no API keys, no rate limits, no
institutional account. Distilled, openly licensed models run on consumer GPUs and
Apple Silicon alike. The only cost of entry is hardware you already have.

**Private.** There is no provider-side log, no harvesting for training, and no
terms-of-service change that can retroactively alter what happens to your
conversations. Your session history is a file on your disk, yours to keep or delete.

**Energy visible.** The Energy Use Indicator shows the watt-hours consumed by every
prompt and the running session total, in real time. Local deployment is what makes
this measurable at all — no cloud system can show you the cost of your own query.

**You stay in control.** You choose the model, when to invoke web research, which
documents form your knowledge layer, and what context carries forward. Search,
reasoning, and retrieval stay separate, explicit steps.

## Conducting

Real work is never one-shot. The dominant pattern — you prompt, AI produces — hands
the whole process to the machine: no reflection, no iteration, no real ownership of
the result.

U.ness is built as an instrument set for working the other way. You break a goal into
steps and direct each one. Every hand-off is your decision. You keep a record of the
judgments that were yours.

The goal is not better AI output. It is better human judgment, powered by AI.

*See the Conduct tab.*

## The name

U.ness is a phonetic play on Eunice, after Eunice Newton Foote (b. 1819), the American
scientist whose 1856 experiments showed that air containing carbon dioxide warms more
under sunlight, and cools more slowly, than ordinary air. She reasoned from this that a
higher concentration of atmospheric CO₂ would raise the Earth's temperature — an early
articulation of what we now understand as the greenhouse effect.

Her paper was read at the 1856 meeting of the American Association for the Advancement
of Science by a male colleague rather than by Foote herself, and her work went
unrecognised in the history of climate science for over a century.

The name marks both her scientific insight and the long failure to credit it.
